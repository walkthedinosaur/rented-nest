Last reviewed 22 October 2026 · Covers England only. This is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer: since 1 May 2026, private renters in England with an assured periodic tenancy can ask their landlord for permission to keep a pet, and the landlord can't refuse without a fair reason. Ask in writing and describe the pet. Your landlord has 28 days to reply in writing, and if they say no they should explain why. A household allergy, a home that's too small for the animal or a building lease that bans pets can be fair reasons; "I don't like pets" usually isn't.

Not in England? Different rules apply – see private residential tenancies in Scotland, Renting Homes in Wales and private renting in Northern Ireland.

What the rules say

From the GOV.UK guide for tenants (checked 22 October 2026):

  • Ask in writing and describe the pet – its type, size and the space it needs (for example, an enclosure).
  • 28 days to reply. Your landlord must respond in writing within 28 days. They can ask for more information about the pet; if you don't give it, they don't have to consider the request. Once you do, they have the rest of the original 28 days or 7 more days, whichever is later, to decide.
  • A fair reason to refuse. They can't say no without one, and should explain a refusal.
  • Permission sticks. Once they agree, they can't change their mind or alter the tenancy to remove permission. Each extra pet needs a new request.
  • Don't skip the asking. Getting a pet without permission may breach your tenancy agreement.

Shelter adds that your landlord "cannot evict you for asking", and that they can't charge more rent in return for saying yes – any rent increase has to go through the normal once-a-year process. For the wider changes, see the Renters' Rights Act in full.

How to make a request that's easy to say yes to

The law only requires a written request that describes the pet, but a landlord is more likely to agree quickly if you answer their worries up front. Shelter says landlords may ask about a pet's health, such as vaccinations, or whether it barks a lot or has been aggressive. Consider including:

  • The basics: species, breed, age, size, and where you're getting it from.
  • Health and care: vaccinations, flea and worm treatment, neutering, microchip – whatever applies.
  • How you'll look after it: who's home when, where it will sleep, dog walking, litter arrangements.
  • A reference from a previous landlord who let you keep a pet, if you have one, and a photo.
  • Damage and cleaning: say you'll pay for cleaning at the end of the tenancy and for any damage the pet causes. Pet insurance is optional – mention it only if you have it.

Send it by email so you have a dated record, and note the date: that's when the 28 days start.

Copy-and-adapt request email

This is a starting point, not a legal document – edit the details in square brackets. Shelter also has its own pet request template.

Subject: Request for permission to keep a pet – [your address]

Dear [landlord or agent's name],

I'm writing to ask for your permission to keep a pet at [address].

The pet is a [species and breed], [age], [size/weight]. [It will live indoors / It will need an enclosure of about X.] It is [vaccinated / microchipped / neutered / treated for fleas and worms – delete as applicable]. I'm getting it from [rescue centre / breeder / family].

[Short paragraph on how you'll care for it – who's at home, exercise, where it will sleep.]

I will pay for professional cleaning at the end of my tenancy and for any damage the pet causes. [I have / plan to take out pet insurance.] [I've attached a reference from my previous landlord and a photo.]

I'm happy to answer any questions. Please could you reply in writing.

Kind regards,
[Your name]
[Date]

Fair and unfair reasons to refuse

GOV.UK gives these examples. They're examples, not a complete list – every case depends on its facts.

Possibly reasonableUsually unreasonable
Someone you live with has an allergyThe landlord doesn't like pets
The home is too small for a large pet or several petsPast tenants' pets caused problems or damage
A freeholder owns the property and doesn't allow pets (common in flats)General worry about future damage or future lets
The pet is illegal to ownYou need an assistance animal, such as a guide dog

If you live in a flat, it's worth checking before you ask whether the building's lease bans pets – the landlord or agent should know, and it's one of the most likely reasons for a fair "no".

If they say no – or don't reply

  1. Ask for the reason in writing if you weren't given one.
  2. Complain to your landlord or letting agent if they haven't replied within 28 days or the reason seems unreasonable – GOV.UK says this is the first step.
  3. Think about a different pet. Shelter says you can choose a different pet and ask again – for example a smaller animal if size was the reason.
  4. Get advice before going further. Shelter says you can ask a court to review the decision, but warns you "might not win" and may need legal help. Talk to Shelter or Citizens Advice first. The new landlord ombudsman isn't running yet (mandatory membership is expected in 2028), so it can't help with pet disputes for now.

FAQ

Can my landlord refuse a pet in the UK?

In England, a landlord with an assured periodic tenancy can't refuse without a fair reason and should explain why. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own rules – see the links at the top of this page.

How long does a landlord have to respond to a pet request?

28 days, in writing. If they ask for more information and you provide it, they have the rest of the 28 days or 7 more days, whichever is later.

Can my landlord make me get pet insurance?

GOV.UK's tenant guide doesn't say a landlord can require it – it says they may be able to claim on your pet insurance if you have it, and can deduct pet damage from your deposit, but not claim twice for the same damage. Not verified beyond GOV.UK's guidance: if a landlord makes insurance a condition, ask Shelter or Citizens Advice.

Can my landlord put the rent up because I have a pet?

Shelter says a landlord can't charge more rent in return for agreeing to a pet. Any rent increase has to follow the normal rules – once a year, with the proper notice.
