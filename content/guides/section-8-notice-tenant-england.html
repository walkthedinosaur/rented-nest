Last reviewed 5 November 2026 · Covers private renters in England only, for notices served from 1 May 2026. This is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer: don't panic, and don't move out yet. A section 8 notice is the start of a process, not an eviction. GOV.UK says your landlord cannot take back possession without a court order, even after the notice ends. Check the notice is on Form 3A and says which ground (reason) your landlord is using, then get free legal advice now – you can get it from the day you receive a written notice.

Not in England? Different rules apply – see private residential tenancies in Scotland, Renting Homes in Wales and private renting in Northern Ireland. Notice served before 1 May 2026? GOV.UK has a separate guide for older notices (Form 3).

What a section 8 notice means

Section 21 "no fault" notices ended on 1 May 2026 (see the Renters' Rights Act explained). Now a private landlord who wants you to leave has to use section 8 and give a ground for possession – a legal reason such as rent arrears, selling the home or moving in themselves.

From the GOV.UK guide for tenants (updated 20 October 2026, checked 5 November 2026):

  • The notice tells you the date from which your landlord can apply to court. It is not a date you must be out by.
  • If you stay after that date, your landlord can apply to court. Only a court can order you to leave.
  • Your landlord must apply to court within 12 months of serving the notice, or it expires.

Shelter's advice is to look for help as soon as you get a notice.

Check your notice

Shelter says a section 8 notice is only valid if it's on the right form, has the right information and gives the right amount of notice. Go through it with this list, then show it to an adviser:

  • Form 3A? Notices from 1 May 2026 should be on Form 3A, or include all the information on it. A letter or text saying "you need to leave" is not a legal notice.
  • Ground and reason given? The notice should list the ground numbers and explain why your landlord is using them – for arrears, how much is owed.
  • Enough notice? The notice period depends on the ground (table below).
  • Deposit protected? Your landlord must protect a deposit in a government-approved scheme within 30 days. GOV.UK says that if they can't show the court this was done (or that it was returned), they generally can't get an eviction order – antisocial behaviour cases are an exception. Our deposit guide names the three schemes to check.
  • Dated and served? Note the day you got it and how (post, by hand, email if your agreement allows). Keep the envelope.

Notice periods for the most common grounds, from GOV.UK's grounds for possession guidance for tenants (checked 5 November 2026):

GroundReasonNotice
1 and 1ALandlord or close family moving in; selling4 months
8Serious rent arrears (mandatory)4 weeks
10 and 11Any arrears; persistent late payment4 weeks
12 and 13Breaking the tenancy terms; damage to the home2 weeks
7A and 14Serious antisocial or criminal behaviourCan apply straight away – no order until 14 days after notice

There are other grounds – check yours against the full GOV.UK list.

What happens next: notice to court

The steps as GOV.UK sets them out. Timings vary by court, so treat this as an order of events rather than a calendar:

  1. Notice period runs. You can stay. Keep paying rent if you can.
  2. Court claim. If your landlord applies, the court sends you their claim and evidence, a defence form and the hearing details.
  3. Defence: 14 days. GOV.UK says "You have 14 days to return the form." This is where you say why you disagree – and you may be able to counterclaim, for example over an unprotected deposit or repairs your landlord hasn't done. Get advice before you fill it in.
  4. Hearing. Go, even if you think you have no defence. The judge may make an outright possession order, a suspended order (you stay if you keep to conditions, such as paying off arrears), adjourn, or dismiss the case. With an outright order, the judge may delay possession by up to 6 weeks if you can show extreme hardship.
  5. Appeal. You normally have up to 21 days to appeal, and you need permission.
  6. Bailiffs. If you haven't left by the date on the order, your landlord can ask for bailiffs. You usually get at least 14 days' notice of the eviction date.

If you lose, the court may order you to pay your landlord's legal costs and any rent owed. That's another reason to get advice early: an adviser may spot a problem with the notice or help you agree a plan before it gets to court.

If it's about rent arrears

Ground 8 is mandatory: GOV.UK says the arrears must be at least 3 months' rent (or 13 weeks' if you pay weekly or fortnightly) both when the notice is given and at the hearing. If you bring the arrears below that by the hearing, the court can't evict you on ground 8 – though your landlord may still rely on grounds 10 or 11, where the judge decides whether eviction is reasonable. GOV.UK also says arrears caused by Universal Credit not yet paid don't count towards the ground 8 threshold – ask an adviser how that applies to you.

  • Talk to your landlord early and offer a realistic payment plan. Get anything agreed in writing.
  • Pay what you can. GOV.UK suggests paying your full rent if your landlord won't agree a plan, or the most you can afford.
  • Get free debt advice from MoneyHelper or Citizens Advice. GOV.UK notes the 12-month limit for applying to court can be extended if you were in a debt "breathing space" scheme.
  • Check benefits: if you claim Universal Credit or Housing Benefit, make sure your housing costs are being paid.

Was the arrears caused by a rent rise you didn't agree to? See whether a rent increase notice is valid.

If your landlord is selling or moving in

Grounds 1 (landlord or close family moving in) and 1A (selling) need 4 months' notice. GOV.UK says they can't be used to start court proceedings in the first 12 months of your tenancy. Your landlord can serve the notice during that year, but it can't run out before the 12 months are up.

These grounds are mandatory if your landlord proves them, so the question is usually whether the reason is genuine and the paperwork is right. An adviser can check. If you'd rather leave on your own terms, you can give your own notice instead – see how much notice you need to give – but get advice first, especially if you may need help from the council.

Where to get free help today

  • Housing Loss Prevention Advice Service: free legal advice from when you receive a written notice, according to GOV.UK. Shelter suggests finding a provider through GOV.UK's legal adviser search, ticking "housing loss prevention advice service".
  • Civil Legal Advice: 0345 345 4345, Monday to Friday 9am–8pm and Saturday 9am–12:30pm, or text "legalaid" to 80010 (as listed on GOV.UK on 5 November 2026).
  • Your council's housing team: GOV.UK lists your local council as a source of help. Contact its housing or homelessness team early, before you agree to leave.
  • Shelter and Citizens Advice for advice on your situation.

Keep everything in one place: the notice and envelope, your tenancy agreement, deposit certificate, rent records and messages with your landlord. An adviser will ask for them.

FAQ

Do I have to leave when the section 8 notice ends?

No. GOV.UK says your landlord cannot take back possession without a court order. The end date is when they can apply to court, not when you must leave.

What is Form 3A?

The official form for section 8 notices served from 1 May 2026. Older notices used Form 3. Shelter says a notice must be on Form 3A or include all its information.

How long does a section 8 notice last?

Your landlord must apply to court within 12 months of serving it, or it expires (with limited extensions for debt breathing space cases).

Can my landlord evict me to sell in the first year?

They can serve notice, but GOV.UK says they can't start court proceedings on the selling or moving-in grounds in the first 12 months of the tenancy.

Can my landlord change the locks or remove my things?

GOV.UK says your landlord can't take back possession without a court order, and evictions are carried out by bailiffs after one. If you're locked out or your belongings are removed, contact your council and Shelter straight away.
