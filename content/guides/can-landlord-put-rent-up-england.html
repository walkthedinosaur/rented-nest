Last reviewed 29 October 2026 · Covers private renters in England only. This is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer: yes, but only once a year, never in the first year of your tenancy, and only with a section 13 notice on the official form (form 4A) giving you at least two months' warning. If your landlord just texts or emails a new figure without that notice, Shelter says you don't have to pay or agree to it. If you think a properly served increase is above the going rate, you can ask a tribunal to set the rent – but you must apply before the new rent's start date.

Not in England? Different rules apply – see private residential tenancies in Scotland, Renting Homes in Wales and private renting in Northern Ireland.

The rules in four lines

From the GOV.UK guide for tenants (checked 29 October 2026), for assured periodic tenancies – which, since 1 May 2026, is what most private tenancies in England are:

  • Once a year. Your landlord can only increase the rent once a year, and the next rise can't take effect until at least a year after the last one did.
  • Not in year one. They can't increase the rent in the first year of your tenancy.
  • Form 4A. They have to use the official notice, form 4A – the "section 13 process".
  • Two months' notice, minimum. And you can ask the First-tier Tribunal to step in if you think the new rent is higher than the open market rent.

GOV.UK doesn't set a percentage cap on rises. The limit is the open market rent – what the landlord could expect to get if they re-let the home on the open market – and that's what the tribunal decides if you challenge. For everything else that changed on 1 May, see the Renters' Rights Act explained.

Is your rent increase notice valid?

Before you decide whether to accept or challenge, check the notice itself. Using GOV.UK's tenant guide, its tribunal guidance and Shelter:

  1. Is it on form 4A? Notices given on or after 1 May 2026 should be on form 4A (the older form 4 was for notices before then). You can compare it with the official form 4A (PDF).
  2. Does it give at least two months? Count from when you received it to the new rent's start date.
  3. Has it been a year? Check the date your last rise took effect – or the date your tenancy started, if you've never had one.
  4. Is the start date right? Shelter says to check paragraph 4 of the notice: the new rent should start on the same day of the month that your tenancy started.
  5. How did it arrive? In person or by post is fine. Email only counts if your tenancy agreement says notices can be sent by email.

If something's wrong – too little notice, a wrong start date, the wrong form – Shelter says to tell your landlord, and you can ask the tribunal to rule on whether the notice is valid. GOV.UK says that if it isn't valid, you don't need to pay the proposed rent; the landlord would have to start again with a new notice.

What if your landlord just texts or emails a new rent?

A message saying "rent's going up to £X from next month" isn't a section 13 notice. Shelter's page on rent increases without a section 13 notice (updated 1 May 2026) says:

  • "You do not need to pay or agree to a rent increase if they do not give you this notice." You can keep paying your current rent.
  • If your landlord or agent pressures you to agree, "it could be harassment".
  • If you're forced to leave because you challenged a rent increase, "it is an illegal eviction".

Shelter has a letter template you can use to tell your landlord about the change in the law. Reply in writing and keep copies. Shelter's legal guidance also says that since 1 May 2026 an agreement to pay more rent isn't valid unless it follows a valid section 13 notice – so even a friendly "is that OK?" should come with the proper form. A landlord can offer you a brand new tenancy at a different rent instead, but you don't have to sign one.

Should you challenge it?

A valid notice isn't automatically a fair one. The question the tribunal asks is whether the proposed rent is above the open market rent for your home. Before you apply:

  • Look at similar homes nearby. Same size, same area, similar condition. GOV.UK says evidence of actual lettings is stronger than letting-agent listings, and Shelter says tenancy agreements for similar local homes are the best evidence – but listings on property portals can still help.
  • Think about the condition. Damp, broken heating or outstanding repairs are relevant. Shelter says you can ask the tribunal to inspect your home – for example to look at damp or repairs. If damp is an issue, our black mould guide covers how to record and report it.
  • Talk to your landlord first. Shelter suggests trying to agree a figure. Get anything you agree in writing, including the start date.
  • Know the downside. For notices given on or after 1 May 2026, Shelter says the tribunal's rent "cannot be more than your landlord asked for" – it could be the same or lower. There's a £47 fee, and the process can take months.

Shelter also says you can't be evicted for challenging a rent increase. And since 1 May 2026 landlords can't use "no fault" section 21 evictions at all.

How to challenge at the tribunal

From GOV.UK's open market rent determination guidance (page updated 10 August 2026) and Shelter's challenge guide, both checked 29 October 2026:

  1. Apply before the new rent starts. GOV.UK: "You must apply before the start date of the new rent given in the notice." Miss it and you can't use this route for that notice.
  2. Use form MR1. Apply online, or email or post the PDF form to the residential property tribunal office for your region. Shelter says to send a copy to your landlord. If you only want to argue that the notice isn't valid, GOV.UK says you must use the paper MR1 form.
  3. Pay the fee or ask for help. It's £47 for notices given on or after 1 May 2026. Help with fees may be available if you're on a low income.
  4. Send your evidence. The notice, your tenancy agreement, photos of every room and the outside, a description of the home and any repairs, and comparable rents.
  5. Wait for the landlord's reply and the decision. GOV.UK says the landlord has 28 days to respond (on form MR2). Shelter says there isn't always a hearing; if there is, it may be online, by phone or in person and may be months away.

When does the new rent start? GOV.UK says that if the tribunal decides before the proposed start date, the new rent usually applies from that date; if it decides after, usually from the next payment date after the decision. If paying the new rent would cause you hardship, you can ask for the date to be put back – you'll need to give full details of your finances. If your tenancy started less than six months ago, the tribunal sets the start date, and it won't be earlier than the date you applied.

The tribunal can't give legal advice. GOV.UK suggests free help from a law centre or Citizens Advice, and Shelter can help too.

Rent review clauses and notices from before 1 May 2026

  • Rent review clauses. Many older tenancy agreements had a clause that put the rent up automatically. GOV.UK says that if an increase was agreed before 1 May 2026 but takes effect after that date, "the increase will not apply". From now on, rises go through form 4A.
  • Form 4 notices served before 1 May 2026. GOV.UK says the notice period and increase in the old form still apply, even if the new rent starts after 1 May – but you can still challenge the rent at the tribunal. GOV.UK says there's no tribunal fee for these. Shelter notes that for these older notices the tribunal's rent could be higher than your landlord asked for, and the new rent normally starts from the date in the notice.
  • Informal rises before 1 May. Shelter says an increase you agreed informally before 1 May 2026 doesn't count in the same way, so a section 13 notice could follow within a year of it.

If you're not sure which rules apply to your notice, ask Shelter or Citizens Advice before the start date passes.

If you can't afford the new rent

  • Check the notice first. An invalid notice means the rise doesn't apply yet.
  • Talk to your landlord early. Ask whether a smaller rise, or a later start date, would work. Get any agreement in writing.
  • Get money advice. Citizens Advice can check whether you're missing out on benefits such as the housing element of Universal Credit, or help with budgeting. If you already get help with housing costs, tell the benefits office about the new rent.
  • Don't stop paying. Keep paying your current rent while you sort things out – arrears are a legal reason for eviction.
  • If you decide to move. You'll normally need to give your landlord proper notice to end the tenancy – check Shelter's guidance before you hand it in. When you go, our move-out deposit checklist helps you get your deposit back.

FAQ

How much notice should a landlord give for a rent increase?

In England, at least two months, on form 4A. Notices given before 1 May 2026 on the old form 4 needed one month.

Do landlords put the rent up every year?

Not automatically – many don't. Since 1 May 2026 the most they can do is raise it once a year, and not in the first year of a tenancy.

Can my landlord increase the rent by text?

No. A text isn't a section 13 notice, and Shelter says you don't need to pay or agree to an increase without one. Email only works for serving a proper form 4A if your tenancy agreement allows email.

Is there a cap on how much rent can go up?

GOV.UK doesn't state a percentage cap. If you challenge, the tribunal decides the open market rent, and for notices since 1 May 2026 Shelter says it can't set a rent higher than your landlord asked for.

Can my landlord evict me for challenging a rent increase?

Shelter says you cannot be evicted for challenging a rent increase, and that being forced out for it is an illegal eviction. Section 21 "no fault" evictions ended on 1 May 2026. If you're threatened, contact Shelter or Citizens Advice straight away.
