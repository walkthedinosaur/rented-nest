Updated November 2026 · Mainly for England. This is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer: pull the wardrobe a few centimetres away from the wall, stop putting anything damp inside it, and photograph the mould before you clean it. Wash or dry-clean the affected clothes, wipe the wardrobe gently, and keep air moving around what's stored inside. If the wall behind it is damp, the wardrobe is built in on a cold outside wall, or the mould keeps coming back, report it to your landlord in writing – finding and fixing the cause is usually their job.

This page is about wardrobes, cupboards and the clothes and shoes inside them. For mould on walls and ceilings, see black mould in a rented flat – what to do. For who's responsible for condensation, see is condensation the landlord's responsibility?

Why wardrobes on outside walls get mouldy

Mould needs moisture and still air. A wardrobe pushed flat against an outside wall gives it both: the wall is the coldest surface in the room, warm damp air condenses on it, and the wardrobe stops air reaching that patch to dry it out. Packed clothes inside, a door that's always shut and a few items put away slightly damp make it worse.

That's why council damp advice keeps coming back to the same few points. South Oxfordshire District Council tells residents to move beds and wardrobes away from external walls by at least 5cm (2 inches), to leave space for air to circulate between walls and furniture, and not to put too many things in cupboards. Adur & Worthing Councils suggest putting wardrobes against internal walls rather than external ones where possible, pulling shelves away from the backs of wardrobes, and not overfilling them so air can circulate around stored clothes.

In a winter flat, the moisture usually comes from everyday life – cooking, showers, breathing and, above all, drying washing indoors. Our guide to drying clothes in a flat without a balcony covers how to keep that moisture out of the bedroom.

Before you clean: photos and a written note

Once it's wiped away, the evidence has gone. Before you clean anything:

  • Photograph the inside of the wardrobe, the back panel, the wall behind it (pull it out first) and any damaged clothes or shoes. Take one wide shot showing where the wardrobe is in the room.
  • List what's damaged and keep receipts if you have to dry-clean or replace things. Shelter says you may be able to claim compensation if damp damages your belongings – you'll need evidence.
  • Note the date, what you found and what you've already tried, such as moving the wardrobe or leaving the door open.

Cleaning mouldy clothes and the wardrobe

Clothes and soft things. Enfield Council's advice is to bag up mouldy soft items and wash clothes at a high temperature, and to shampoo or dry-clean soft furnishings. Adur & Worthing Councils say to dry-clean mildewed clothes. Check each care label first – not everything can take a hot wash – and make sure everything is completely dry before it goes back in the wardrobe. Putting clothes away slightly damp is one of the easiest ways to start the cycle again.

The wardrobe itself. Enfield Council's method for small patches of mould works here too:

  1. Wear long rubber gloves, goggles and a mask that covers your nose and mouth.
  2. Take everything out, open the window and keep the bedroom door shut.
  3. Wipe gently with a cloth dipped in water and mild detergent such as washing-up liquid – the council says this works as well as specialist mould cleaners. Don't brush the mould, and Adur & Worthing say not to vacuum it either: both can spread spores.
  4. Dry the surfaces with a clean cloth, then bag and bin the cloths.
  5. Leave the doors open and let it dry fully before you put anything back.

If the mould is on the wall behind the wardrobe, covers a large area, or someone in the home has asthma, allergies or a weakened immune system, don't tackle it yourself – report it. The NHS says some people are more sensitive to damp and mould, including babies and children, older people, people with skin problems such as eczema, people with breathing problems such as allergies and asthma, and people with a weakened immune system, and that they should stay away from damp and mould.

Stopping it coming back – without drilling

  • Leave a gap. At least 5cm between the wardrobe and an outside wall, as South Oxfordshire suggests – more if you can. If there's an internal wall free, move it there.
  • Let air in. Leave the doors ajar for part of the day, especially in the morning when you open the windows. Don't pack it full.
  • Nothing damp goes in. Not freshly ironed shirts that are still warm, not shoes you've just walked home in from the rain, not a gym bag.
  • Keep the room's humidity down. Dry washing somewhere else, with a window open, not on the bedroom radiator. If the bedroom stays damp, a small dehumidifier in the room does far more than anything you put inside the wardrobe.
  • Keep the bedroom reasonably warm. A very cold room makes condensation more likely. If the heating isn't working, see no heating or hot water – what to do.

When it's your landlord's job

You're expected to do everyday things to keep moisture down. But mould in a wardrobe often points to something about the home itself, and then it's usually your landlord's job to find the cause and fix it:

  • The wall behind the wardrobe is damp, stained or cold to the touch even when the wardrobe has been pulled out and the room is heated and aired.
  • The wardrobe is built in on an outside wall, so you can't move it or leave a gap. Don't try to remove or alter fitted furniture yourself – ask.
  • There's a leak from a pipe, window, roof or gutter, or damp coming through the wall.
  • The mould keeps coming back however careful you are, or the heating or extractor fans don't work.

Report it in writing with your photos and readings – our condensation guide explains how and what to include. Shelter says your landlord must try to find the cause, and should get a damp surveyor if they can't, which you don't have to pay for. If they ignore it, your council can inspect – see how to make your landlord act. For how common damp is in rented homes, see the official numbers.

Can I move the landlord's wardrobe? Pulling a freestanding wardrobe a few centimetres off the wall is a reasonable, reversible thing to do. Moving it to another room, taking it apart or unscrewing anything fixed to the wall is different – ask first, in writing, so it doesn't become a deposit argument later.

FAQ

Do moisture absorbers work in a wardrobe?

They collect some water from the air in a small closed space, which can help protect clothes and shoes. They won't fix a damp wall, a cold outside wall or a damp room – for that you need airflow, a gap from the wall, lower humidity in the room, and the landlord to fix any defect.

Is mould in a wardrobe the landlord's responsibility?

It depends on the cause. Everyday condensation habits are partly on you, but if the wall is damp, there's a leak, the wardrobe is built in on a cold wall, or it keeps coming back despite airing and heating, the cause is usually the landlord's to find and fix. More in is condensation the landlord's responsibility?

Can I save clothes with mould on them?

Often, yes – washing at a high temperature where the care label allows, or dry-cleaning, is what council advice suggests. Make sure they're fully dry before they go back. Keep receipts if you pay for cleaning or replacements.

How far should a wardrobe be from the wall?

South Oxfordshire District Council suggests at least 5cm (2 inches) from an external wall. More helps if you have the room.
