### 1. Challenging a cleaning fee request after vacating a flat *(10+ replies, 23 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6687819/challenging-a-cleaning-fee-request-after-vacating-a-flat
Son left a flat after 8 years, family cleaned it; agent wants £194 for a professional clean over dust in the extractor
vent, mould in the bath sealant and dirt between the oven door glass. The agent has already raised a claim with the
deposit scheme.

**Draft answer:** Don't agree to the deduction – if the deposit was protected, the landlord or agent has to prove the
flat was left dirtier than at check-in, allowing for fair wear and tear and for how long someone lived there. After
eight years, some wear is expected, and deposit schemes generally won't let a landlord charge for a full professional
clean just to get back to "new". Gather your evidence now: the check-in inventory (if there was one – without it the
landlord's case is much weaker), your own move-out photos, and receipts for cleaning products or a cleaner. Then
respond to the scheme's dispute with that evidence and say which items you accept, if any (a dusty vent might be a
small amount; tired bath sealant after 8 years is normally wear and tear). The scheme's adjudication is free and the
money stays protected while it's decided. (General information, not legal advice.)

### 2. Problems with return of rental deposit *(10+ replies, 29 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6688575/problems-with-return-of-rental-deposit
Relative moved out of a two-bed bungalow after four years; landlord is keeping the £1,100 deposit for stained carpets
and "a full redecoration". They paid a £100/month pet surcharge for a dog. Deposit is with a protection scheme.

**Draft answer:** A landlord can claim for damage beyond fair wear and tear, but not for making the place like new.
After four years, carpets and paintwork will usually need some refreshing anyway, so even where there's real damage,
schemes tend to award a share based on how old the carpet or decoration was – not the full cost of replacing it. Ask
the landlord to itemise the deduction with quotes or invoices, and the age of the carpet and when the place was last
decorated. If you don't agree, use the deposit scheme's free dispute service rather than negotiating forever – you
need to raise it within the scheme's time limit. The pet surcharge you paid doesn't by itself cancel a claim for pet
damage, but it's worth mentioning. Check-in and check-out reports and photos are what decide these cases.

### 3. Can't claim back deposit from DPS *(1 reply, 21 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6687540/cant-claim-back-deposit-from-dps
An old Ilford tenancy ended a few years ago; the agent says the £700 deposit was never claimed. Poster has the DPS
deposit ID but can't find the tenancy in their account or any way to claim it.

**Draft answer:** It's worth chasing – deposits that are never paid out don't just disappear. First check which scheme
actually holds it (the deposit ID and the prescribed information you got at the start should say; it might be a
different scheme from the one you're looking in, or an "insured" deposit the agent holds itself). If it's with DPS,
use their online contact form/help pages rather than the tenant dashboard, give the deposit ID, the property address,
the tenancy dates and your ID, and ask how to recover an unclaimed deposit. Ask the agent in writing to confirm the
scheme, the deposit ID and that they haven't been paid it. Keep everything in writing.

### 4. 30% rent increase? *(10+ replies, 9 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6686202/30-rent-increase
Tenant of 8 years, landlord raising the rent 30% "citing market value"; counter-offer rejected; tenant unwell and
facing surgery, wants more time.

**Draft answer:** In England, since 1 May 2026 a landlord can only raise the rent once a year, on the official form
(form 4A, the "section 13" notice), with at least two months' notice – a text or email naming a new figure isn't
enough, and you can keep paying the current rent until a valid notice arrives. There's no percentage cap; the limit is
the open market rent for your home in its actual condition. If you think 30% is above that, you can apply to the
First-tier Tribunal (form MR1, £47 fee for new notices) – but you must apply before the new rent's start date. For
notices since 1 May the tribunal can't set a rent higher than the landlord asked for. Gather rents of genuinely similar
homes nearby (same size and condition) and photos of any disrepair. If paying would cause hardship, you can ask the
tribunal to delay the start date. And keep paying your current rent meanwhile – arrears are a ground for eviction.

### 5. Rent increase after 11 years at same level *(30+ replies, 11 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6686386/rent-increase-after-11-years-at-same-level
11 years in a three-bed house with no rent rise, doing minor repairs informally; the landlord (abroad, may sell) has
asked the tenant to propose a "fair figure".

**Draft answer:** You're in a good position – a landlord who asks you to suggest a figure wants to keep you. Look at
what similar homes nearby actually rent for, then adjust down for anything that's tired or that you've been fixing
yourself (list the repairs you've done – that work has value to him). A sensible offer is often below the full market
rate in return for a reliable long-term tenant and no void period or agent fees. Once you agree, the increase should be
done properly on form 4A (in England, since 1 May 2026 an informal agreement to pay more isn't valid on its own), and
you can then ask to put the repairs arrangement in writing too.

### 6. Should I continue to pay rent when a tenancy is under dispute? *(7 replies, 24 Oct)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6687972/should-i-continue-to-pay-rent-when-a-tenancy-is-under-dispute
Signed for a house they never moved into because it "stunk of cigarette smoke" and was dirty; disputing the tenancy
and deposit; asks whether to pay next month's rent and whether paying means accepting the tenancy.

**Draft answer:** Be careful about stopping rent on your own: once you've signed, the tenancy exists whether or not you
moved in, and unpaid rent becomes arrears that can be claimed from you or the deposit. The safer route is to put your
complaint in writing straight away (photos of the condition, dates you reported it) and ask the landlord to agree to
end the tenancy early by mutual agreement – a "surrender" in writing. If the house was genuinely unfit, the council's
private housing team can inspect. Get advice from Shelter or Citizens Advice before you withhold anything, because the
answer depends on the exact wording of what you signed. Don't rely on an AI tool's guess for this one.

### 7. Rent with no income but large savings – Renters' Rights laws? *(9 pages, first post Dec 2025, still active)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6644885/rent-with-no-income-but-large-savings-renters-rights-laws
Long-term tenant, not working for two years but with large savings, worries about renting again when the tenancy ends
in December 2026; asks whether savings income counts and whether to tell the landlord.

**Draft answer:** Many landlords and referencing firms accept savings instead of a salary – the usual test is whether
you can show enough to cover the rent for the length of the tenancy, with bank statements. Interest and dividends can
count as income on an application. What's changed since 1 May 2026 in England is that landlords can't ask for months
of rent up front in the way some used to, so you may be asked for a guarantor or a rent guarantee product instead. Be
honest on applications (misleading a landlord can come back on you); lead with the savings evidence, a reference from
your current landlord and your rent history.

### 8. Rights as a tenant *(10+ replies)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6613116/rights-as-a-tenant
Family of three: deposit called an "advance payment" and not protected, leaking roof, damp and mould, rent dispute;
landlord suggested they "look for alternative accommodation".

**Draft answer:** A deposit is still a deposit even if it's called an "advance payment" – in England it must be
protected in a government-backed scheme within 30 days and you should have been given the scheme details. If it
wasn't, that's a serious breach you can raise (Shelter explains how to claim compensation). Leaks, damp and mould are
the landlord's repairs: report them in writing with photos and dates, and if nothing happens, contact the council's
private housing team. Since 1 May 2026 landlords can't use "no fault" evictions, and being told to look elsewhere is
not an eviction notice. Get Shelter or Citizens Advice to look at the whole picture.

### 9. How often is too often for an inspection when renting? *(landlord's question, 10+ replies, 5 Nov)*
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6689417/how-often-is-too-often-for-an-inspection-when-renting
A Birmingham landlord's agent now suggests quarterly inspections; landlord asks what's reasonable. Renters ask the
same thing from the other side.

**Draft answer (tenant view):** There's no fixed legal number, but inspections should be reasonable and your landlord
must give at least 24 hours' written notice for a visit (except genuine emergencies) – you have the right to live in
the home without unnecessary disturbance. Every 3–6 months is common; more than that, with no repair reason, can feel
intrusive. If quarterly visits bother you, say so in writing and suggest six-monthly, or ask for photos of a specific
issue instead of a full walk-round. A landlord can't let themselves in without agreement outside an emergency.

### 10. One present for a whole household *(141 replies, Mumsnet, Nov 2025 – seasonal, last year)*
https://www.mumsnet.com/talk/christmas/5440784-one-present-for-a-whole-household
A relative wants one gift per household; poster needs ideas for two parents and children aged 3, 6 and 10.

**Draft answer:** Go for something the household does together: a family board or card game that works across ages,
a cinema-night box (popcorn, hot chocolate, a film voucher), a big jigsaw, or a day-out voucher. For a small rented
home, avoid anything that needs wall space or assembly – a game or an experience takes up no room at all.
