The short answer: wrap a soft tape measure around the widest part of your head – about a finger's width above your eyebrows, just above your ears and around the bump at the back – over your hair as you'd wear it to ride. Measure two or three times and take the number in centimetres. Then check it against that brand's size chart, because sizes and head shapes vary between makes. The measurement gets you to the right size; a correct fit still needs trying on, and both the BHS and BETA recommend a fitting by a BETA-trained hat fitter.

A riding hat only protects you properly if it fits and stays in place. Here's how to measure, how to check the fit, and when to get help.

How to measure your head, step by step

  1. Get a soft tape measure – a fabric dressmaker's tape, not a metal DIY one. A helper makes it easier.
  2. Do your hair as you ride. Naylors and Charles Owen both suggest a low ponytail. Rydale adds that if you have very thick or long hair, you may need to size up.
  3. Find the widest part of your head. Charles Owen's guide puts the tape about a finger's width above your eyebrows, just above the ear line and around the bump at the back of your head. Naylors describes the same line.
  4. Keep the tape level and snug, not tight, and read it in centimetres.
  5. Repeat two or three times and use the most consistent figure.

Turning centimetres into a hat size

UK riding hats are usually sold in centimetres, traditional hat sizes (like 6⅞ or 7) and, for skull caps, numbered sizes (like 1, 2, 3). For example, Charles Owen's chart lists 57cm as a size 7 helmet or a size 2 skull cap, and Naylors' conversion table runs from 49cm (6 / 000) up to 61cm (7½ / 4). Always use the chart for the exact hat you're looking at – brands don't all match.

  • Between sizes? Charles Owen suggests rounding up to the larger size, or ideally trying on both.
  • Head shape matters as much as size. Most heads are oval, but some are rounder. Rydale's rule of thumb: tight at the forehead but loose at the sides suggests the hat is too round for you; the reverse suggests it's too oval. Naylors says that if a hat doesn't suit your shape, try a different model before changing size. Some brands make "round fit" versions.
  • Children: don't buy a size too big to grow into – both Charles Owen and Rydale warn against this. The BHS notes children's hats can differ in shape from adult hats of the same brand.

How to check the fit

The retailer and manufacturer guides we read (Charles Owen, Naylors, Rydale, Eland Lodge) agree on the basic checks:

  • Put it on front to back: forehead in first, then roll it down at the back until you feel it "lock" onto the back of your head.
  • Level and low enough: the front sits roughly a finger's width to an inch above your eyebrows.
  • Even, snug pressure all round with no gaps or sore spots.
  • The rock test: with the harness undone, the hat shouldn't rock or slide when you gently move it.
  • Chin strap last: adjust the harness only once the shell fits. The strap sits just under your chin with about one finger's gap, and the side straps should gently touch your earlobes.
  • Wear it for at least five minutes before deciding – pressure points and headaches often only show up after a while.

If a hat gives you a headache or pressure point, Eland Lodge's advice is to take it back to a BETA-trained retailer rather than living with it.

Get a professional fitting and buy new

Measuring at home is a good start, but it's not the whole job. What the safety bodies say (both pages checked 21 November 2026):

  • BHS (Hats & helmets, last reviewed 2 January 2026): it's "essential that your hat is secured and fits properly"; have it fitted by a certified hat fitter with BETA training; avoid second-hand hats; look for a quality assurance mark – such as a BSI Kitemark or SEI mark – alongside the safety standard label; and replace your hat after a fall, especially if it hit the ground. It also says not to dry hats on a radiator and to keep them out of long periods of direct sun.
  • BETA (Safety and your head): get a free, personalised hat fitting at a BETA-trained retailer, and never buy or borrow a second-hand hat with an unknown history.
  • The law and competitions: the BHS notes Highway Code Rule 49 – children under 14 must wear a compliant helmet when riding on the road, and other riders should follow the same advice. Competitions follow each discipline's own hat rules, and BETA publishes a hat rules table – check the current one for your sport.

We haven't listed specific safety standards here because the accepted list changes; check the label and your discipline's current rules, or ask your fitter. Naylors recommends buying from BETA-registered brands and avoiding seconds, used hats and cycle or motorbike helmets.

Buying online? It works best when you're replacing a hat you've already been fitted for, in the same make, model and size. Horse & Hound reported in 2015 that Champion supported online sales only for like-for-like replacements of a correctly fitted hat.
