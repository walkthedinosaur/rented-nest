The short answer: the main difference is length. Jodhpurs are full-length riding trousers that reach the ankle and are worn with short jodhpur or paddock boots. Breeches finish around the lower calf so they can tuck into long riding boots (or be worn with short boots and half chaps). Riding tights are a newer, pull-on, legging-style version that works with either kind of boot. For most beginners, jodhpurs or riding tights are the cheaper and easier first pair.

All three are designed for the saddle: close-fitting, stretchy, with smooth inside seams so they don't rub, and usually with extra grip on the knee or seat. Here's how to choose.

Jodhpurs, breeches and riding tights compared

  • Jodhpurs – full length to the ankle. Your Horse says they often have a cuff that can be rolled up, and Naylors explains the extra fabric at the ankle is why they don't sit flat inside a long boot. Traditionally they're cotton-based and some have an elastic strap under the boot or use jodhpur clips to stop them riding up. Worn with jodhpur or paddock boots.
  • Breeches – cut to finish just above the ankle or mid-calf and sit flat on the leg, so they fit inside tall boots without bulk. Your Horse says most modern breeches have an elasticated cuff, and some use touch-and-close fastening. They're often made of technical, stretchy fabrics. Worn with long boots, or short boots plus half chaps.
  • Riding tights – full-length pull-on leggings in very stretchy fabric, usually with a high waist and silicone grip. Harry Hall's guide says they work with either boot type.

Your Horse sums it up: the "only real difference" between jodhpurs and breeches is where they end on the leg. Both are fine for riding and for yard work.

Knee patch or full seat?

Whichever style you choose, you'll also pick a grip type. Naylors' guide explains the trade-off:

  • Knee patch – grip only on the inside of the knee. It keeps your lower leg still but is less restrictive; Naylors says it's particularly popular with people who jump.
  • Full seat (sometimes called a "sticky bum") – grip over the whole area that touches the saddle. It helps keep you still from the hip down, which some riders like on spooky or big-moving horses, but Naylors notes it can make it harder to keep a rhythm in rising trot. It says these are most popular for hacking and dressage.
  • Silicone vs suede or fabric patches – silicone grip is less bulky but needs care when washing and drying. Cheaper traditional jodhpurs may have a fabric "self" patch or a suede-effect knee instead, according to Eland Lodge.

New riders: a knee patch is a sensible first choice – it's usually cheaper and less restrictive while you're learning to rise to the trot. This is a general pointer, not a rule; ask your instructor if unsure.

Which boots go with which?

Children grow fast, so cheaper jodhpurs and tights make sense for kids; Eland Lodge notes jodhpurs are generally the traditional choice for younger riders.

Which should you buy?

  • Beginners and children: jodhpurs or riding tights with jodhpur boots – the cheapest full set-up, and the boots double as yard boots. See what to wear for your first riding lesson.
  • Regular riders who want a longer boot look: breeches with short boots and half chaps – cheaper than long boots and easy to swap.
  • Competing: check your discipline's or club's rules for dress. Eland Lodge notes breeches are the usual choice in the competition arena and jodhpurs are used for showing; we haven't verified individual rule books.
  • Winter: look for thermal or fleece-lined versions of any style.

Whatever you choose, try a few on: sizing varies between brands, some use UK dress sizes and others waist inches. Sit down and lift your knee when you try them – they shouldn't pull at the waist or bag at the knee.
