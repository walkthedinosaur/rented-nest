The short answer: for your first riding lesson you need very little of your own. Wear close-fitting, stretchy trousers (leggings or jodhpurs, not jeans), a few thin layers, a waterproof if the lesson might be outdoors, and sturdy boots with a small heel. Tie long hair back low and take off jewellery. Most riding centres can lend you a riding hat – and often boots – but check when you book. Don't buy a hat until you've been properly fitted.

That's the advice from British Equestrian's "Your first lesson" page and the British Horse Society (BHS). Below is the detail for each item, what to borrow versus buy, and a sensible order to buy kit in if you decide to keep riding.

Riding hat: borrow first, then get fitted

A riding hat is the one piece of kit you must have. British Equestrian says "most riding centres will be able to provide you with one, but check at the time of booking", and staff may fit you for a hat when you arrive.

If you decide to buy your own later, the BHS hats page (last reviewed 2 January 2026, checked 14 November 2026) says:

  • Your hat should meet one of the accepted safety standards. A hat with a quality assurance mark, such as a BSI Kitemark or SEI mark, can be viewed as safer than one made to the same standard without one.
  • Have it fitted at a reputable tack shop by a fitter with appropriate BETA training, and try several – head shapes differ. Children's hats can be shaped differently from adult hats, even in the same brand.
  • Don't buy second-hand: damage can't always be seen with the naked eye. BETA's hats page (checked 14 November 2026) also says it is "never a good idea to purchase or borrow a second-hand one with an unknown history".
  • Replace a hat after a fall, especially if it hit the ground.

Your riding school may require a particular standard – ask before you buy. On UK roads, the Highway Code (Rule 49, as quoted by the BHS) says children under 14 must wear a helmet that complies with the regulations. We haven't listed specific hats here, because fit matters more than brand: BETA suggests visiting a BETA-trained retailer for a free hat fitting.

Long hair: British Equestrian suggests a braid or low ponytail that sits comfortably below the hat – a high ponytail or bun can stop the hat fitting properly.

Boots: a small heel matters most

British Equestrian recommends "boots with a small heel to prevent your foot slipping through the stirrup", and says centres may lend you a suitable pair. Naylors' first-lesson guide adds that boots shouldn't be overly grippy, and that some schools will let you wear trainers for your first few lessons – but check first, because trainers have no heel.

Trousers, tops and layers

British Equestrian advises "close-fitting clothing that allow ease of movement" and layers you can take off if you get too warm. Naylors' guide says trousers should be "not baggy" and to avoid rough materials, because your legs rub against the saddle – which is why jeans, with their thick inside seams, are best avoided.

  • Trousers: stretchy leggings or tracksuit bottoms are fine to start with. If you keep riding, a pair of jodhpurs or riding tights is the first thing worth buying – they have smooth seams and often grip patches on the knee or seat. See our guide to jodhpurs vs breeches.
  • Top half: thin layers – a T-shirt, a fleece or jumper – rather than one bulky coat.
  • Waterproof: British Equestrian notes some lessons are outdoors, so dress for the weather. A short jacket is easier in the saddle than a long one.
  • No jewellery: British Equestrian says remove it for safety, as it could catch on something.

Gloves and body protectors

Gloves are optional for a first lesson, but Naylors' guide says they help prevent rubs and give a better grip on the reins; ask the school whether ordinary gloves are OK. Riding gloves are thinner than winter gloves so you can feel the reins – Mark Todd Adult Leather Riding Gloves were £29.98 at GS Equestrian, and cheaper synthetic pairs are widely available.

{{awin:49229|https://gsequestrian.co.uk/products/mark-todd-leather-riding-show-gloves-adult|Mark Todd leather gloves at GS Equestrian}} {{amazon:horse riding gloves|Shop on Amazon UK}}

Body protectors aren't usually needed for a first flatwork lesson, though Naylors notes they're recommended for jumping and cross-country, and some nervous beginners find one reassuring. The BHS body protectors page (last reviewed 1 October 2026, checked 14 November 2026) says most are designed to meet EN13158 and a BETA level standard, and recommends Level 3 for the most protection. It also says a body protector must fit correctly and should ideally be fitted by someone with BETA training. Many schools lend them – ask, and don't buy one without a fitting.

What to buy first if you keep riding

British Equestrian points out that riding clothes don't have to be expensive – specialist online shops, sports and outdoor shops and some supermarkets all sell them. A sensible order:

  1. Jodhpurs or riding tights – the cheapest upgrade and the one you'll notice most (from around £10–£20 at the prices above).
  2. Jodhpur or paddock boots – once you know you're carrying on (from about £17 for children and £29 for adults at the prices above).
  3. Gloves – a basic pair for grip and to save your hands.
  4. Your own hat – bought new and fitted by a BETA-trained fitter, to the standard your school or club asks for.
  5. Body protector – when you start jumping or hacking out, or if your school asks for one, again professionally fitted.

The BHS suggests choosing a BHS Approved Centre for lessons, saying they "go the extra mile to ensure the highest levels of safety" and that their staff hold qualifications – you can search for one on the BHS website. Arrive early: British Equestrian says you'll fill in a rider registration form, including any medical conditions your coach should know about.
