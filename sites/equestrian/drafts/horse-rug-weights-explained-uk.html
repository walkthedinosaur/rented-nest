Updated October 2026 · General information, not veterinary advice. If you're unsure about rugging a particular horse, speak to your vet.

The short answer: a rug's "weight" is the amount of polyester fill inside it, measured in grams (per square metre). As a rough guide, the British Horse Society describes lightweight as 0–100g, mediumweight as 150–300g and heavyweight as 350g and over. But there's no single industry standard – retailers and brands draw the lines in slightly different places – so always read the gram figure, not just the word on the label. And the right weight depends on the horse, not the calendar: many horses are comfortable without a rug at all until it gets properly cold, wet or windy.

Here's what the numbers mean, why they vary, what else on the label matters, and how to check whether the rug you've chosen is actually right.

What the grams mean: lightweight, medium and heavy

The fill is the padding sandwiched between the waterproof outer and the lining. More grams means more insulation. Rugs with no fill at all (often sold as "0g" or "no fill") are waterproof shells that keep off rain and wind without adding much warmth.

Here's how three UK sources split the bands (all checked 30 October 2026):

  • British Horse Society: light 0–100g, medium 150–300g, heavy 350g+.
  • The Saddlery Shop (retailer guide): lightweight up to about 150g, medium 150–250g, heavyweight 250–400g.
  • Equus (retailer guide): fills run from 0g up to about 450g for the coldest weather, with no fixed gram figure per band.

So a "medium" from one brand could be 150g and from another 250g. That's a big difference on a mild, damp night. When you compare rugs, compare the gram figure in the product name or spec.

Turnout or stable rug – and why liners help

Turnout rugs have a waterproof, tougher outer for the field. Stable rugs are usually quilted and not waterproof – they're for indoors only. The same gram figures apply to both, but a stabled horse out of the wind and rain usually needs less than the same horse out in the field in bad weather.

Instead of owning every weight, many owners use a liner: a thin fill rug that sits under an existing rug and adds, say, 100g or 200g. A lightweight turnout plus a liner can cover a lot of the year – just check the liner is compatible with your rug and doesn't cause rubbing.

Denier, necks and the other numbers on the label

Denier (D) describes how heavy and tough the outer fabric's yarn is – it's about strength, not warmth. Equus's guide lists 600D, 1200D and 2100D outers and suggests 1200D or higher for horses that are playful or hard on their rugs; a cheaper 600D rug may wear out sooner. A higher denier doesn't make a rug warmer.

Neck style also changes how warm a rug is:

  • Standard neck – stops at the withers. Suits milder weather and horses that run hot.
  • Combo (fixed) neck – full neck cover built in.
  • Detachable neck – lets you switch between the two as the weather changes.
  • High neck – sits a little higher to take pressure off the chest and withers.

World Horse Welfare points out that a native type with a thick mane may not need a neck cover at all, and one can rub the mane or make the horse too hot.

Measuring for size is its own job – roughly, from the centre of the chest, around the widest part of the shoulder, along the side to the point of the buttock, then compare with that brand's size chart, because sizes vary between brands.

How to tell if your horse needs a rug at all

The BHS says many horses are comfortable without a rug until temperatures fall below about 5°C, and that prolonged rain and wind chill a horse more than dry cold. World Horse Welfare says horses can usually regulate their own temperature between about 5°C and 25°C. Both stress that it depends on the individual. Things that change the answer include:

  • Body condition – horses carrying more fat are better insulated; the BHS warns against unnecessary rugging of overweight horses, as it may add to the risk of weight gain and laminitis.
  • Coat and type – healthy natives and cobs with shelter and plenty of forage often cope without one; finer-coated types may not.
  • Clipping – a clipped horse has lost some of its own insulation.
  • Age and health – older, younger, underweight or unwell horses may need more help. Your vet is the person to ask.
  • Forage, shelter and movement – digesting forage makes heat, and hedges, trees or a field shelter make a big difference.

Horses find it easier to warm up than to cool down, so the BHS advice is to rug only when needed and, if you can't change rugs during the day, choose the lightest suitable option.

Checking the weight is right

The BHS's quick check: slide a hand under the rug behind the withers. Your horse should feel comfortably warm and dry – not cold, and not damp or sweaty.

Signs of being too hot (BHS): sweating under the rug, heavy breathing, lethargy or irritability, frequent rubbing, warm damp skin. Hidden sweat also makes both the coat and the rug less effective.

Signs of being too cold (BHS): shivering, a tightly clamped tail, seeking shelter, pacing and gradual weight loss.

Take the rug off every day to check for rubs or sore spots, and refit or change it if needed. If your horse shows signs of being unwell, or you're not sure what they need, speak to your vet.

Building a rug collection from scratch? A lightweight turnout, a mediumweight, and a liner covers more weather than you might expect – add a heavyweight only if your horse actually shows signs of being cold.
