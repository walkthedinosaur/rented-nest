The short answer: book an assessment or "returners" lesson at a riding centre approved by a British Equestrian member body – the BHS, the Association of British Riding Schools (ABRS), the Pony Club or Riding for the Disabled (RDA). Tell them honestly how long it's been and how confident you feel, so they can match you to the right horse. Wear comfortable trousers and boots with a small heel; most centres will lend a hat at first. Once you know you're sticking with it, buy your own hat, fitted in a shop by a BETA-trained fitter, and then add boots, half chaps and a body protector.

Lots of riders come back after years away – often once children are older or there's finally time and money. Here's how to make the return go smoothly.

Find an approved riding centre

British Equestrian's "Return to equestrianism" page (checked 28 November 2026) says "the best place to start back is at a riding centre", and recommends one approved by one of its member bodies – the ABRS, BHS, Pony Club or RDA. Approved centres are inspected against their body's standards.

  • Search the lists. The BHS "Find a riding centre" search lets you filter by riding school, trekking centre and more. The BHS notes its list "may not include real-time updates", so check a centre's status with the BHS if in doubt.
  • Be honest when you book. British Equestrian advises telling the centre your real ability so they can match you to a horse or pony that suits your height, weight and experience.
  • Riders with a disability: British Equestrian says the RDA has more than 500 groups in the UK, and some centres hold an RDA Accessibility Mark.

Lessons for returners: what to expect

According to British Equestrian, many approved centres run structured adult courses – it names examples such as "Take Back the Reins", "Rusty Riders" and "Adult Returners" – and many will book you an assessment lesson first, then move you into a group at the right level. Private or semi-private lessons are usually available too.

  • Group lessons are usually cheaper and sociable; you'll ride with people at a similar level.
  • Private lessons give one-to-one attention – useful if confidence is the main hurdle – and can cost more per session (prices vary a lot by area; ask the centre).
  • What next: British Equestrian says some centres offer part-loans or shares of their horses, and most offer horse care training – a good way to rebuild stable skills before thinking about your own horse's kit.

Fitness and confidence

Riding works muscles you may not have used for years. British Equestrian points out that it uses your back, thighs and legs in particular, and says: "Don't be surprised if you feel sore after your first session." Naylors' returning-rider guide suggests building up the frequency and length of sessions slowly to avoid injury.

Nerves are normal, especially if you remember riding fearlessly as a teenager. British Equestrian's view is that "with the right horse and support" returning riders can get going again quickly. Practical things that help:

  • Ask for a calm, experienced school horse for your first few lessons.
  • Start with a private or small-group session and say what worries you.
  • Expect things to have changed – instructors may teach position or aids differently from how you learned.
  • If you have a health condition or past injury, check with your GP before getting back in the saddle.

Kit: hat first, then boots, then the rest

Your riding hat. If you still have your old hat, don't use it – it's almost certainly out of date, and you can't see damage inside one. The BHS hats page (checked 28 November 2026, last reviewed 2 January 2026) says:

  • it "always recommends" buying a new hat from a reputable tack shop, fitted by someone with BETA training;
  • it advises against second-hand hats "as it's impossible to see any damage with the naked eye" – BETA's hats page (checked the same day) says the same about borrowing one with an unknown history;
  • you should replace a hat after a fall, "especially if your hat comes into contact with the ground", and dropping it can reduce its protection too;
  • a hat must meet the rules of wherever you ride or compete – approved centres and organisations publish their own hat rules each year, so ask the centre what they accept before you buy. We haven't listed specific standards here; check the current rules with the centre or the hat fitter.

A body protector. Many centres require one for jumping and some for hacking. BETA's body protector page (checked 28 November 2026; undated, refers to rule changes from January 2024) says the BETA 2018 standard meets the European standard EN 13158, and that Level 3 is for "general riding, competitions including eventing and working with horses". It recommends a free fitting at a BETA-trained retailer, replacing a body protector "at least every three to five years", and avoiding second-hand ones.

Boots. The BHS says boots should have a smooth sole and a small heel ("no less than ½ inch high"), and calls trainers and wellies unsuitable. Short jodhpur boots are the usual first buy – they work at the yard too. Add half chaps to protect your calves. See long riding boots vs jodhpur boots and half chaps.

A simple returner's checklist

  • Find a BHS, ABRS, Pony Club or RDA approved centre near you
  • Book an assessment or adult returners lesson – be honest about your experience
  • First lessons: comfortable trousers, boots with a small heel, borrowed hat if offered
  • Expect to be sore – build up gradually, and check with your GP if you have a health concern
  • Sticking with it? Get a new hat fitted by a BETA-trained fitter
  • Add jodhpur boots and half chaps, then a BETA 2018 Level 3 body protector if your centre needs one
  • Ask about horse care sessions, part-loans or shares when you're ready

If you go on to share or loan a horse, ask your vet about anything to do with that horse's health or welfare.
