Updated November 2026 · General information, not veterinary advice. If you're unsure what a particular horse needs, speak to your vet.

The short answer: a turnout rug is a waterproof rug made for horses that are outside – in the field, paddock or track. A stable rug is made for indoors: the British Horse Society describes it as a rug or sheet "used indoors, often made from cotton or fleece", and it isn't built to keep out rain. A turnout rug can usually be worn in the stable too, but a stable rug shouldn't go out in the field. Which you need depends on how your horse is kept – and plenty of horses need neither for much of the year.

Here's what each type is for, when one rug can do both jobs, and the other rugs you'll see in the shops.

Turnout rugs: built for the field

A turnout rug has a waterproof outer – Kramer's guide says lightweights are mostly synthetic fibres such as polyester with a water-resistant coating – made from tougher material than a stable rug so it can cope with outdoor life. Two numbers matter on the label:

  • Fill (grams) – how warm it is. The BHS bands are lightweight 0–100g, mediumweight 150–300g and heavyweight 350g+, though brands draw the lines slightly differently. A 0g turnout is just a waterproof shell. (More in our guide to horse rug weights.)
  • Denier – how strong the outer fabric is. The BHS says the higher the denier, the stronger the material and the better its water resistance. It isn't a warmth rating.

Many turnouts also come with leg straps or a fillet string to help keep the rug in place, and a choice of neck styles (standard, combo or detachable).

Stable rugs: for horses indoors

Stable rugs aren't waterproof. Many are quilted (you'll often see "channel quilt" in product names), and some have a smooth outer so bedding doesn't cling to them. They come in the same range of fills, from thin sheets with no fill to heavyweights of 400g or more.

Two things to keep in mind:

  • A stabled horse is out of the wind and rain, which is a big part of what makes horses cold outside. So the same horse often needs a lighter rug – or no rug – indoors than out.
  • Overnight, horses make their own heat by digesting forage. World Horse Welfare gives the example of an unclipped, stabled horse with forage all night in a heavyweight rug with a neck in mild weather as one that could overheat.

Can one rug do both jobs?

Turnout in the stable – usually yes. Many owners leave the turnout on when the horse comes in. Online for Equine's guide says turnout rugs are "perfectly suitable to use both in the field and in the stable", and that one rug saves time on the yard. The catch is that a rug that's right for a cold, wet field may be too warm in a sheltered stable, so check your horse rather than assume.

Stable rug in the field – no. Retailer guides such as Kramer's describe stable rugs as only suitable for the stable. Without a waterproof outer, a stable rug gets soaked, a wet rug stops insulating, and it isn't made to take the knocks of turnout.

Why some owners keep both: changing into a separate stable rug means the horse goes into a clean, dry layer for the night, gives you a chance to groom and check for rubs, and lets a wet or muddy turnout dry out before the morning.

If your horse lives out all the time, you don't need a stable rug at all. If they live in most of the time, you may not need a turnout. Buy for how your horse is actually kept.

Other rugs you'll see – and what they're for

The BHS lists several other types. None of these replaces a turnout or stable rug:

  • Cooler – "a thin, breathable rug used after exercise to help a sweaty horse dry without getting chilled in cold weather" (BHS). Fleece coolers are common.
  • Exercise sheet – worn while riding, lungeing or long-reining to keep the hindquarters warm and dry. Some are high-visibility for roads.
  • Travel rug – a light sheet for the lorry or trailer; the BHS says to check the horse isn't overheating.
  • Liner – a thin fill layer worn under another rug to add warmth without buying another full rug. Check it's compatible with your rug.
  • Fly rug – for spring, summer and early autumn, against biting insects.

So which do you need?

Start with whether your horse needs a rug at all. The BHS says many horses stay comfortable without one until temperatures drop below about 5°C, and that wet, windy weather chills horses more than dry cold. World Horse Welfare says horses can usually regulate their body temperature between about 5°C and 25°C. Horses more likely to need help include finer-coated types, clipped, older, underweight or unwell horses. Shelter and plenty of forage come before adding rug weight.

Then match the rug to the routine:

  • Lives out: turnout rug(s) only, if needed.
  • Out by day, in at night: a turnout for the field, and either the same rug overnight or a lighter stable rug.
  • Mostly stabled: a stable rug if needed, plus a turnout for any time out in bad weather.
  • Clipped and in work: may also benefit from an exercise sheet and a cooler.

Whatever you choose, the BHS advises taking the rug off every day to check for rubbing or sore spots, and using the hand check: slide a hand under the rug behind the withers – your horse should feel warm and dry, not cold or sweaty. Signs of being too hot include sweating and heavy breathing; too cold, shivering and a clamped tail. If you can't change rugs during the day, choose the lightest suitable option. If you're unsure, or your horse seems unwell, speak to your vet.

Fit matters as much as type: measure from the centre of the chest, around the widest part of the shoulder, to the point of the buttock, and check each brand's size chart. See our guide on how to measure a horse for a rug.
