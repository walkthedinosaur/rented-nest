The short answer: for most leisure riders, jodhpur boots plus half chaps are the cheaper, more flexible choice – you can walk around the yard in the boots, and the half chaps give your lower leg the grip and protection a long boot would. Long boots give a taller, stiffer leg that many riders find steadier, and they're the usual choice for adults at affiliated competitions. Whichever you pick, the safety basics are the same: a smooth sole with no break from heel to toe, and a small heel.

Here's how the two set-ups compare, and what to check before you buy.

The safety basics apply to both

The BHS footwear page (checked 28 November 2026, last reviewed 28 August 2026) says "traditional riding boots or jodhpur boots" are the best choice, and that boots should:

  • have a smooth sole running all the way from heel to toe, so nothing can catch on the stirrup;
  • have a small heel – the BHS says "no less than ½ inch high" – which stops your foot sliding through the stirrup;
  • suit your stirrups: the BHS suggests about a ½ inch gap between the stirrup iron and the widest part of your foot.

The BHS calls wellies and trainers "unsuitable for riding", and warns that heavily treaded yard boots can catch in the stirrup if you fall. So the choice below is really about comfort, feel and budget – both long boots and jodhpur boots can be safe if they meet those points. Riding schools may lend you boots for lessons; if you're just starting out, see what to wear for your first riding lesson.

Jodhpur boots with half chaps or gaiters

Jodhpur boots are short, ankle-height riding boots – usually pull-on with elastic sides, or zipped. On their own they leave your calf against the saddle flap and stirrup leather, which can pinch. Half chaps fix that: they wrap the lower leg from ankle to just below the knee and, as Decathlon's buying guide puts it, do "the same role as the upper would on longer boots" – stopping rubbing and adding grip.

Why riders like this set-up:

  • Flexibility and comfort. Kramer's guide lists "maximum flexibility" and comfort around the stable as the main pluses – you can take the half chaps off and do yard jobs in the boots.
  • Cost. Kramer says the combination is "usually a little cheaper than long leather riding boots"; the prices below bear that out.
  • Awkward leg sizes. Separate half chaps are easier to fit if your calves are wider, slimmer, shorter or longer than standard boot sizes.

Half chaps vs gaiters: half chaps are usually synthetic or suede and casual; gaiters are leather, shaped to look like the top of a long boot, and cost a bit more. Millbry Hill's guide says gaiters are "sometimes allowed in competition" while half chaps are "typically not" – check the rules for your class (see below). Decathlon suggests synthetic half chaps for beginners (light and easy to care for) and leather for more experienced riders, noting leather needs regular care.

Long riding boots

Long boots come up to just below the knee in one piece. Kramer's guide points to the "high and stable shaft" as the main advantage, and says the flexibility of short boots and half chaps "requires good riding skills" to keep a steady lower leg – so some riders find long boots help their leg position. Millbry Hill describes three styles: field boots (laced at the front of the ankle, popular for jumping), dress boots (plain, usually black) and hunt boots (with a contrasting top). Most now have a zip at the back rather than being pull-on.

The downsides: they cost more, they're less practical for walking about the yard, and a stiff leather boot can take time to soften. They also need a good calf fit, which is harder to get off the shelf – look for brands sold in several calf widths.

Sizing: measure your calf, not just your foot

For half chaps, gaiters or long boots you need two calf measurements as well as your shoe size. Kramer's guide suggests:

  • Height: for half chaps and gaiters, from the middle of the ankle to just below the back of the knee (long boots are measured differently – follow each brand's size guide).
  • Width: around the widest part of your calf.
  • Measure standing, later in the day (legs swell a little), wearing the breeches and socks you ride in. If you're between sizes, Kramer says "choose the next size up".
  • Half chaps should finish about two finger-widths below the back of the knee and sit close without squeezing or wrinkling.

Don't lever your boots off with the heel of the other foot – Kramer says it damages them. Loosen them, or use a bootjack.

Which should you choose?

  • New, returning or weekly riders: jodhpur boots plus synthetic half chaps – cheaper, comfortable, and the boots double as yard footwear. See getting back into riding as an adult.
  • Riders with hard-to-fit calves: jodhpur boots with half chaps, or long boots from a brand with several calf widths.
  • Competing: check your rulebook before buying. Millbry Hill describes long boots as standard adult competition footwear and says leather gaiters are sometimes accepted where half chaps aren't – rules vary by organisation and class, so go by the current rules for your event (not verified for any specific body).
  • Want a steadier-feeling leg: try long boots, or leather gaiters for a similar look and feel on a smaller budget.

Whatever you buy, check it against the BHS basics: smooth sole, small heel and the right stirrup size. Pair your boots with the right legwear – see jodhpurs vs breeches.
