Damp and mould in rented homes: the official numbers (England)

How common is damp in private rented homes? The English Housing Survey figures in plain English – damp by tenure, types of damp, cold homes – and what to do if yours is affected.

Last reviewed 22 October 2026 · Covers England only. Figures from the English Housing Survey 2024 to 2025 (published 29 January 2026). General information, not legal advice.

The short answer: about 1 in 10 private rented homes in England (10%) had a damp problem in 2024, according to the government's English Housing Survey – more than social rented homes (7%) or owner-occupied homes (4%). Across all tenures, that's 5% of homes, or about 1.4 million. When renters themselves are asked, the figure is much higher: 46% of private renting households said their home had problems with condensation, damp or mould.

If your rented home has damp, it isn't unusual – but it isn't something you just have to live with either. Below is what the survey found, what the numbers mean, and what to do next.

Is it getting worse? For owner-occupied homes (2% to 4%) and social rented homes (5% to 7%), the survey reports a significant increase since 2022. For private rented homes the figure went from 9% in 2022 to 10% in 2024, but the report says this "apparent rise" was not statistically significant – so the honest reading is "roughly 1 in 10, and not clearly changing", not "damp is rising in rentals".

Private rented homes were also more likely to have more than one type of damp at once: 2.9%, against 1.2% of social rented and 0.7% of owner-occupied homes.

For comparison, about 2% of owner-occupied homes and 5% of social rented homes had serious condensation or mould. Penetrating and rising damp were each around 1–2% in the other tenures. Why renters report far more damp than surveyors find

The same survey also asks households directly. In 2024, 30% of all households said their home had problems with condensation, damp or mould – and private renters were the most likely to say so (46%, against 41% of social renters and 22% of owner occupiers).

Why the gap between 46% and 10%? The two numbers measure different things:

  • The 10% is a trained surveyor's judgement that a home has a damp problem – penetrating damp, rising damp, or serious condensation or mould.
  • The 46% is what households say, and includes everyday condensation – wet windows on winter mornings, a steamy bathroom – that may never become a surveyed damp problem, as well as damp that a single survey visit could miss.

Neither number is "wrong". Everyday condensation is common and can usually be managed; persistent damp or mould that comes back after you've ventilated is the kind to report.

A separate 2024 poll for Citizens Advice (YouGov, 2,015 private renters in England, 12–14 June 2024 – a poll, not official statistics) found 45% were experiencing damp, mould or excess cold. Of renters who had lived with these problems, 37% had never complained to their landlord, and 51% of those gave fear of retaliation, such as eviction or a rent increase, as the reason. That poll was taken before the Renters' Rights Act ended "no fault" evictions in England.

Ventilation is the strongest link

Only about 1% of homes had inadequate ventilation – but 60% of those had a problem with damp. If moist air from cooking, showers and drying clothes has nowhere to go, it ends up on the coldest walls and windows.

Working ventilation – extractor fans, trickle vents, windows that open – is usually the landlord's part; using it is yours. Cold homes and energy efficiency

Cold walls make condensation worse, so warmth and damp go together. The survey found:

  • Excess cold: 3% of private rented homes had an excess cold hazard serious enough to count as a Category 1 hazard (the most serious level), the same share as owner-occupied homes and well above social rented homes (0.3%).
  • Any Category 1 hazard: 10% of private rented homes in 2024 (12% in 2022); 3% – about 125,000 homes – had more than one.
  • Energy efficiency: the average private rented home scored 66 on the government's SAP energy rating in 2024, up from 64 in 2019. 48% were in energy efficiency band C, 37% in band D (down from 43% in 2022) and 9% in band E.

What's changing

  • Awaab's Law for private renters: it already sets repair deadlines for damp and mould hazards in social housing. A government consultation on extending it to private rented tenancies and licences was published on 9 October 2026 and closes at 11:59pm on 18 December 2026. There's no start date for private renters yet.
  • Decent Homes Standard: the government's January 2026 consultation response says a reformed standard will apply to private and social rented homes from 2035.
  • Renters' Rights Act: since 1 May 2026, section 21 "no fault" evictions have ended in England – see what the Renters' Rights Act changed.

We'll update this page when the government responds to the Awaab's Law consultation and when the next English Housing Survey housing quality figures are published.

If your home has damp: four steps

  1. Report it in writing to your landlord or letting agent – email is fine – and keep a copy.
  2. Keep evidence: dated photos, humidity readings, and a note of damaged belongings and any effect on health.
  3. Contact your council's environmental health team if nothing happens or the problem is serious. It can inspect and can require a landlord to act.
  4. Get free advice from Shelter or Citizens Advice for your own situation.

About these figures

The English Housing Survey is run for the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government. The 2024 to 2025 housing quality findings combine physical surveys of homes with household interviews and were published on 29 January 2026; the condition figures above are for 2024. Percentages are as published – we haven't recalculated or rounded them. The survey covers England only: Scotland has the Scottish House Condition Survey, and Wales and Northern Ireland run their own house condition surveys.
