Best dehumidifiers for rented flats (UK)

Streaming windows every morning, a musty wardrobe, washing that takes three days to dry on the airer – it's a familiar winter in a lot of UK rented flats. A dehumidifier won't fix a leak or a broken extractor fan, but it can take a lot of moisture out of the air, cut condensation and make drying clothes indoors far less grim.

Most decent machines cost somewhere between £100 and £400, so it's worth getting the type and size right first. Here's how to choose, a shortlist of models to look at, and what to do if the damp is your landlord's problem rather than yours.

How to choose a dehumidifier for a flat

Compressor or desiccant. According to Which?, compressor (refrigerant) dehumidifiers work by creating a cold surface that moisture condenses on, and typically struggle in colder spaces. Desiccant models work in warm and cold rooms alike, so they suit an unheated flat, a chilly bedroom or a room you only heat for an hour or two.

Size. Capacity is quoted in litres extracted per day. Which? groups them roughly as 7–12 litres for small spaces, 14–16 litres for medium and 18 litres or more for large. A one- or two-bed flat with ordinary condensation is usually fine with 10–12 litres; go bigger if you dry a lot of washing indoors.

Laundry mode. Nice to have, not essential. Which? notes that any dehumidifier can help dry washing, and many "laundry modes" simply run the machine at full power.

Tank size and continuous drain. A bigger tank means fewer trips to the sink. If you can sit the unit near a sink, shower tray or drain, a continuous-drain hose outlet means you never empty it at all.

Noise. In a studio or one-bed you'll hear it. Look for a quiet or night mode and check owner reviews for noise before you buy.

Running costs. According to Which?, its Best Buys average around 7p an hour to run, with the most efficient around 3p and the least efficient about 17p (at 26.32p per kWh). A built-in humidistat, which switches the machine off once the room reaches your target, helps keep that down.

A cheap digital hygrometer (humidity meter) tells you whether you need a dehumidifier at all – and whether the one you've got is doing its job.

Damp in a rental: what's on you and what's on your landlord

Day-to-day condensation is something you're generally expected to help manage – open windows or trickle vents when you can, use extractor fans when cooking and showering, and keep furniture a little away from cold outside walls. A dehumidifier fits in here.

But if damp or mould keeps coming back despite that, or it's caused by something like a leak, a faulty extractor fan, broken heating or rotten window frames, finding and fixing the cause is usually your landlord's job – not yours, and not something a dehumidifier should be papering over. You're not alone if it happens: the government's English Housing Survey found a damp problem in about 1 in 10 private rented homes in England in 2024 (damp in rented homes: the official numbers).

  • Report damp and mould to your landlord or letting agent in writing, and keep a copy.
  • Take dated photos, and note humidity readings if you have a hygrometer.
  • Keep a record of every call, email and message, and let them in to inspect.
  • If nothing happens and the problem is serious, Shelter suggests contacting your council's environmental health team.

Rules differ between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland and do change, so check current advice from Shelter or Citizens Advice for where you live before you take things further. For England, our guide Is condensation the landlord's responsibility? sets out who does what and how to report it.

Wipe condensation off windows and sills every morning with a squeegee or cloth. It takes a minute and stops water soaking into frames and sealant – which also helps at check-out.
