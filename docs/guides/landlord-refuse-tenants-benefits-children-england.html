Can a landlord refuse tenants on benefits or with children? (England)

Since 1 May 2026 "No DSS" and "no kids" are against the rules for private lets in England. What landlords and agents can't do, what they can still check (affordability, a few exceptions), what to say, and how to report it.

Last reviewed 19 November 2026 · Covers private renters in England only. This is general information, not legal advice.

The short answer: no – not just because you get benefits or have children. Since 1 May 2026, landlords and letting agents in England can't discourage or stop you from renting a private home for either reason. That covers "No DSS" or "professionals only" adverts, refusing viewings, and clauses in tenancy agreements, mortgages or leases that ban benefit claimants or children – those clauses can't be enforced. A landlord can still check whether you can afford the rent, but must count benefits as income. There are a few narrow exceptions for children (such as overcrowding), and councils can fine landlords and agents up to £7,000 per breach.

This is one of the changes from the Renters' Rights Act – see what the Renters' Rights Act changed for renters for the rest.

Not in England? Different rules apply – Scotland has its own rental discrimination guidance; for Wales and Northern Ireland see Renting Homes in Wales and private renting in Northern Ireland.

What landlords and agents can't do

From GOV.UK's rental discrimination guide for landlords (first published 1 May 2026) and Shelter's "no benefits" discrimination page (updated 1 May 2026), both checked 19 November 2026. A landlord, or anyone acting for them – letting agents, referencing companies, even friends or family – can't:

  • Advertise "No DSS", "no benefits" or "professionals only", or say they don't rent to people on benefits (Shelter).
  • Refuse or block a viewing because you get benefits or have children – even if you can afford the rent.
  • Hold back information about a property, such as whether it's available, when, its size or number of bedrooms.
  • Refuse to let to you, or offer worse tenancy terms, for either reason.
  • Treat you differently later – for example if you start getting benefits or have a baby after you move in.
  • Rely on a ban in a mortgage, lease or tenancy agreement. GOV.UK says those terms aren't valid and can't be enforced.

It covers children under 18 who live with you or visit, and a long list of benefits including Universal Credit, Jobseeker's Allowance, PIP, ESA, Pension Credit, Council Tax Support, Child Benefit and Carer's Allowance. The rules apply to private assured tenancies in England; social and supported housing aren't covered. Discrimination because of a protected characteristic such as race, disability or religion is separately unlawful under the Equality Act – see GOV.UK: discrimination, your rights.

What they can still check: affordability and the exceptions

Affordability. A landlord can still refuse someone who genuinely can't afford the rent. But GOV.UK says that if they check affordability, benefit income must be counted the same way as other income. Shelter adds that they can look at your full costs – rent plus bills, insurance and council tax. GOV.UK gives an example of a referencing company that ignored a tenant's benefit income and rejected them for "not earning enough": the council treated it as discrimination and fined the landlord.

Benefits – no general exception. GOV.UK says there's no "legitimate aim" exception for refusing people on benefits. The only one is insurance: a landlord can rely on an insurance contract that bans benefit claimants only if it started before 1 May 2026 and hasn't renewed since. It ends when the contract ends or renews, and you can ask to see it.

Children – a few exceptions. GOV.UK's page on renters with children lists situations where a landlord may be able to say no:

  • shared homes where unrelated adults share space, such as student housing;
  • a property that's too small, where a child would make it overcrowded;
  • where the landlord has to meet certain licensing conditions;
  • safeguarding concerns that can't reasonably be dealt with.

To use one, the landlord has to show it's a "proportionate means of achieving a legitimate aim" – explaining why the restriction is necessary and why it applies to your situation. A blanket "no children under 16" with no reason doesn't do that: GOV.UK's own example of exactly that ended in a fine. The same pre-1 May 2026 insurance exception applies to children too.

If you don't have a payslip at all, see no payslip? Options when renting – landlords can no longer ask for months of rent up front instead.

If it happens: keep evidence and ask for the reason

The council will look at evidence, so save it before it disappears:

  • Screenshot the advert with the date and the website showing.
  • Keep every message – texts, emails, WhatsApps, portal messages. After a phone call, write down the date, who you spoke to and what they said.
  • Note what happened next – a cancelled viewing, a "the property's gone" that stayed listed, a referencing rejection.

Then ask, politely and in writing, for the reason. A clear written answer either solves the problem or becomes evidence.

Subject: My application for [property address]

Dear [agent or landlord's name],

On [date] I [asked to view / applied for] [address]. [I mentioned that I receive Universal Credit / that I have two children], and [the viewing was cancelled / I was told the property isn't suitable / my application was declined].

How to report it

GOV.UK's page on what happens when someone reports rental discrimination (checked 19 November 2026) says you can:

  1. Contact your local council. It can ask the landlord for details, give them 28 days to respond with evidence, and then issue a fine of up to £7,000 to each person responsible – including an agent acting for the landlord. If the problem carries on, it can fine again every 28 days; a repeat within five years can mean a bigger penalty. The landlord can appeal to the First-tier Tribunal.
  2. Complain to the letting agent's redress scheme, if an agency was involved. Agents usually name their scheme on their website or paperwork. Redress schemes can order remedies such as compensation or an apology.
  3. Take your own court action. Courts can also order compensation or an apology. Get advice from Shelter or Citizens Advice before going down this route.

A council fine punishes the landlord or agent; it won't necessarily get you the flat. If you're also facing homelessness, tell your council's housing team – that's a separate duty and shouldn't wait for a complaint.

FAQ

Is "No DSS" illegal in England?

Yes, for private assured tenancies since 1 May 2026. Shelter lists "No DSS", "no benefits" and "professionals only" adverts as against the law.

Can a landlord still ask for a guarantor because I'm on benefits?

GOV.UK's discrimination guidance we checked doesn't cover guarantors, so this is not verified. What it does say is that benefits must be counted like any other income. If you're asked for a guarantor only because you claim benefits, ask Shelter or Citizens Advice whether that's allowed.

Can a landlord refuse because my mortgage or insurance says "no benefits"?

Not because of the mortgage – GOV.UK says those mortgage terms can't be enforced. Insurance only counts if the policy started before 1 May 2026 and hasn't been renewed, and you can ask to see it.

My landlord says the flat is too small for my family. Is that allowed?

It can be, if a child would make it overcrowded – that's one of GOV.UK's listed exceptions. The landlord still has to explain why it's necessary in your case. If the reasoning doesn't add up, ask for it in writing and get advice.

Does this cover house shares and lodgers?

Shared homes where unrelated adults share space are one of the listed exceptions for children. The rules apply to assured tenancies, so lodgers living with their landlord aren't covered in the same way. Check with Shelter if you're unsure.
