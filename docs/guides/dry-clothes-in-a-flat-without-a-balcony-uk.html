How to dry clothes in a flat without a balcony (UK)

No garden, no balcony, no tumble dryer? How UK renters can dry washing indoors without streaming windows, mould or a big energy bill.

Updated October 2026

No balcony, no garden and no tumble dryer is the normal set-up in a lot of UK flats, and from October to March most washing ends up drying indoors. The short answer: spin it hard, dry it in one small room with the door shut, keep it off the radiators, and either ventilate or run a dehumidifier next to it. Below is a routine that works in a rented flat, the kit worth buying, and roughly what each option costs to run.

The quick routine that works

Every load of washing puts litres of water into the air. In a small flat it ends up on the coldest surfaces – windows, outside walls, the back of the wardrobe – which is how condensation turns into mould. This order of steps keeps most of that water under control:

  • Extra spin first. Which? recommends an extra spin cycle before drying indoors. The less water in the clothes, the less goes into the room.
  • Pick one room and shut the door. A bathroom with an extractor fan, or the smallest room you don't sleep in, is ideal. Drying in the bedroom means breathing damp air all night.
  • Space things out. Leave gaps between items and turn thick things halfway through. Crammed rails dry slowly and smell musty.
  • Ventilate or dehumidify. Crack a window or run the extractor fan, or close up the room and run a dehumidifier beside the airer (see below) – not both at once.
  • Wipe the windows in the morning. A squeegee or cloth on the glass and sills stops water soaking into frames and sealant.

Keep washing off the radiators

Draping clothes over radiators is the obvious move, but it works against you. Which? advises against it: the moisture goes straight into the room and settles as condensation, and covering the radiator cuts the heat it gives out. In a rental, damp patches and mould around windows can also become a deposit issue at check-out.

If you must use the radiator, use it for the last half hour on one or two nearly-dry items, and keep a window open in that room. Never put clothes on a plug-in heater – it's a fire risk.

Speed it up: dehumidifier, heated airer or both?

A plain airer on its own is free but slow, especially in a cold flat. Two buys make the biggest difference:

  • A dehumidifier pulls the water out of the air instead of letting it settle on the walls. In a Which? test with two identical loads, using a dehumidifier almost halved the drying time, and pulled around 2 litres of water out of the air. Which? says its Best Buy dehumidifiers cost around 7p an hour to run.
  • A heated airer warms the rails gently. Which? estimated around 7p an hour – roughly £55 a year for three small loads a week, based on September 2025 prices.

For comparison, Which? puts a tumble dryer at around 33p a load for a heat pump model and 82p–89p for condenser and vented models – but many small flats simply don't have room for one. If you do have one, Which? says an efficient heat pump dryer is likely to be cheaper than running a dehumidifier for hours.

A dehumidifier with a laundry mode

Many compressor dehumidifiers have a laundry setting that runs at full power until the room is dry. Which?'s tips: put it close to the airer without the clothes touching it, at least 20cm from walls and furniture, with the door and windows shut. Keeping damp out of a rented flat

Drying washing indoors is unavoidable for many renters, and government guidance for landlords in England is clear that damp and mould shouldn't be blamed on "lifestyle choices" such as drying laundry. Your part is the everyday habits above; if mould keeps coming back anyway, that points to a problem with ventilation, heating or the building, which is generally for your landlord to sort out.
